DECISIONS ALREADY MADE (Adilet, after discussion with Sultan)
1. Scope: all sectors. Strategic products plus food, energy and chemicals, construction
   materials, medical supplies.
2. Research question: two parts with equal weight. (1) How much is replaced and how fast.
   (2) Who replaces, and why them.
3. Definition of a "big" supplier:
   - Main rule: at least 50% of UAE imports of the product.
   - Candidate screen: 20%.
   - Every result is shown at 20, 30, 40 and 50%, so nothing rests on one number.
   - Persistence (from Sultan's rule): the share must pass the threshold in the year before
     the drop AND in at least one of the two years before that.
   - Sultan's HHI > 0.4 alternative was NOT adopted. Adilet kept only the share rule.
4. A drop: the supplier's share falls 15 points or more AND its sales fall at least a third
   (log change <= -0.4), in a product worth $30m or more in the year before.
5. A shock is dated from official sources (decrees, sanctions orders, disaster dates). It is
   never picked because imports fell. The data screen only suggests candidates.
6. Comparison group: the six Gulf importers (ARE, SAU, OMN, KWT, QAT, BHR) as the main group,
   run at the product x country level. World importers from BACI as a backup check.
7. Hypotheses (all four kept):
   H1. In the short run, other suppliers replace only a small part of the loss.
   H2. Food is replaced faster than other goods. This was narrowed from "commodities" because
       fuels and metals turned out to be no faster.
   H3. The replacers were already selling to the UAE before the shock.
   H4. The UAE replaces faster than other Gulf importers (trade hub with more suppliers).
       Not tested yet.
8. Model: simple intuition only. Buyers treat goods from different countries as close but not
   perfect substitutes (CES / Armington), and switching suppliers has a cost.
9. Roles, split by data track:
   - Sultan: annual screen at 20/30/40/50%, final case list, dating each shock from official
     sources, literature review and proposal, who-replaces regression with gravity data (H3).
   - Adilet: monthly data for the case products and all six Gulf importers, event studies,
     Gulf comparison with the world check (H1, H2, H4), figures and replication files.
   - Both: robustness, writing, final presentation. Weekly meetings.
10. Seminar deadlines are not known yet. The timeline (charts/08_timeline.png) is tentative.

METHOD (three steps)
- Step 1. Event study per shock (descriptive, H1 and H2). Monthly imports from the hit
  supplier and each alternative, 12 months before to 24 after. Supplier-product effects and
  calendar-month effects. PPML in levels so zero months stay in.
- Step 2. Gulf comparison (causal, H1 and H4). Exports of alternative j to Gulf importer d of
  product k, on Exposure(dk) x Post, with j-k-time effects and d-time effects. Exposure = the
  hit supplier's pre-shock share of d's imports of k. Add a UAE x Exposure x Post term for H4.
- Step 3. Who replaces (H3). Gain in UAE share regressed on prior share, world exports,
  distance, trade agreement, unit price, with case effects. Gravity data from CEPII.
- Inference: few shocks, so case-by-case results, clustering by supplier-product, and a wild
  cluster bootstrap or randomization inference.

DATA (all collected, checked and in the repository)
- CEPII BACI HS92 v202601: annual 1995-2024 (use 1995-2023, since 2024 is built from partner
  reports only).
- UN Comtrade: monthly exports to the UAE reported by about 30 supplier countries, Jan 2010 to
  Sep 2026, 97 product groups. Also monthly exports to all six Gulf importers for the case
  products. The UAE itself does not publish monthly imports by country.
- Eurostat Comext DS-045409: monthly EU and UK exports to the UAE and the Gulf, 1988 to Aug 2026.
- SIPRI and OECD BaTIS: background only.

KEY FACTS (all verified against the data)
- UAE goods imports were about $390bn in 2023 (BACI).
- In 20 of 59 strategic products, one supplier holds more than half of UAE imports. In 2023,
  China had 79% of computers, Brazil 77% of poultry, India 74% of rice, and Saudi Arabia 55% of
  polyethylene.
- China's share of all UAE imports rose from about 5% (late 1990s) to about 20% (2023).
- Reliability: BACI is within about 10% of UAE-reported totals in most years. Partner ranking
  correlation is 0.98+. Monthly supplier data summed to years has a median ratio of 0.99 to
  BACI. The 30 monthly reporters cover about two thirds of UAE imports.
- 2013-16: the UAE booked about a third of its imports as "unspecified" (chapter 99). Those
  years are flagged.
- Japan earthquake 2011: Japan's car shipments to the UAE fell 74% in Apr-May 2011. US
  shipments stayed flat (+0.7%) and EU car exports to the UAE fell 21%, so nobody filled the gap
  in those months. Over 2011, Korean cars to the UAE went from $327m to $784m and US cars rose 22%.
- India wheat ban (May 2022): India fell from 43% of UAE wheat (2022) to 0.5% (2023). Russia
  gained 23 points.
- India onion ban (Dec 2023 - May 2024, window Dec-Apr): India -89%, from 19.6 to 2.1 kt a month.
  Pakistan rose from 2.3 to 10.9 kt and China from 7.1 to 10.3 kt.
- Iran 2018-19: Iran's share of semi-finished steel went from 71% to 11%.
- Iran 2012: Iran's polyethylene share went from 46% to 7%, and Saudi Arabia gained 28 points.
- Ukraine 2022: sunflower oil share 57% to 36%, pig iron 65% to 4%.
- Rejected cases: India rice ban (the UAE got an exemption), Shanghai lockdown 2022 (no drop),
  Qatar blockade (the UAE's own decision), Australian alumina 2020 (new UAE refinery), Red Sea
  2024 (EU exports kept rising, but that test is weak).
- First look at the hypotheses. Sample: supplier-wide drops, 1996-2023 excluding 2013-16.
  - H1: by the next year, others make up a median 4-11% of the loss, at every cut-off.
  - H2: food about 24% made up, other groups about 4-5% (20% cut-off).
  - H3: in 93% of drops, the biggest gainer was already selling to the UAE the year before
    (median share about 5%).
  - These are descriptive medians, not causal results.
- Where the cases fall under the big-supplier rule:
  - 50% at HS4: Japan trucks, Iran steel 2019, Indonesia palm oil, Ukraine sunflower oil,
    Ukraine pig iron.
  - Japan cars: only 30% overall (38% in 2010), but 50% for small cars (HS 870322, 64%).
  - India onions: 20% overall, 40% for fresh onions.
  - India wheat: fails every threshold with 2019-21 as pre-years (1%, 4%, 34%). It passes at
    30% with 2020-22.
  - Iran polyethylene 2012: 30%.
- Identification checks already run (audit/validity_*):
  - The drops for Japan cars, India wheat and India onions are worse than in 98-100% of other
    months for the same pair. Palm oil is within normal noise.
  - In 6 of 7 cases the supplier sold less everywhere, so the cause is on the supply side.
    Iran 2012 is the exception: only the UAE stopped buying.
  - Weight fell as much as value, so the drops are real quantities, not price moves.
  - Anticipation: wheat shipments were up 67% in the months before the drop (contract rush),
    and onion exports were already down 48% after the August 2023 duty.
  - Exposure is correlated with pre-shock growth across the six Gulf importers (-0.81 to +0.52).

Slide 1. Title (Sultan, 20 sec)
- On the slide: "When a big supplier fails: how the UAE replaces lost imports." Adilet and

Slide 2. Proposal in one slide (Sultan, 1 min)
- On the slide:
  - Question: when a big supplier is hit by an outside shock, how much of the lost supply is
    replaced and how fast, and who replaces it.
  - Approach: shocks dated from official sources. We follow monthly imports before and after,
    compare the UAE with the other Gulf importers, and explain who wins.
  - Data: CEPII BACI (yearly, 1995-2023), UN Comtrade (monthly, 2010-2026), Eurostat (monthly).
    Already collected and checked.
  - What's new: the importer side, for a small trade hub, across many shocks and sectors.
- Say: "The data is ready and we have a first look. What we need to settle is the right model
  and comparison group."

Slide 3. Motivation (Adilet, 1 min)
- On the slide:
  - The UAE imports about $390bn of goods a year (2023) and can't make most of it at home.
  - In 20 of 59 strategic products, one country supplies more than half. For example, China
    has 79% of computers, Brazil 77% of poultry and India 74% of rice.
  - China's share of all UAE imports went from about 5% (late 1990s) to about 20% (2023).
  - Shocks keep happening: the Japan earthquake (2011), Indian export bans (2022-24), the
    Ukraine war (2022), sanctions on Iran (2012, 2018-19).
- Say: "Governments talk a lot about supply-chain risk. Whether a lost supplier gets replaced
  quickly or slowly tells us how costly depending on one country really is."
- He may ask: "Why the UAE and not the whole Gulf?" Answer: it's the most trade-dependent and a
  re-export hub. We use the rest of the Gulf as the comparison group.

Slide 4. Hypotheses (Sultan, 1.5 min)
- On the slide, four lines, each naming where it comes from:
  - H1: in the short run, other suppliers replace only a small part of the loss. From Boehm
    et al. (2019) and Monarch (2022): switching suppliers is slow and costly.
  - H2: food is replaced faster than other goods. From Broda and Weinstein (2006): wheat from
    one country is close to wheat from another, which isn't true of cars.
  - H3: the replacers already sold to the UAE before the shock. From Monarch (2022): buyers go
    to suppliers they already know.
  - H4: the UAE replaces faster than other Gulf importers, because a trade hub has more
    suppliers. This one is our own.
- Say: "The first three come from the papers. The fourth is about the UAE specifically, and
  only our Gulf comparison can test it."
- He may ask: "Why only food in H2?" Answer: we started with commodities in general, but the
  data shows fuels and metals are no faster. So we narrowed it, and we can say why.

Slide 5. Literature (Sultan, 2 min)
- On the slide, three boxes, each with one line on what we take:
  - Supply shocks and switching: Freund et al. (2022) and Boehm et al. (2019) on the 2011
    earthquake, plus Monarch (2022). They give us H1 and H3, and the idea that pre-shock
    exposure predicts switching.
  - Bans and sanctions: Giordani, Rocha and Ruta (2016) show bans follow world prices, so we
    control for them. Haidar (2017) shows sanctioned goods find other routes, so we check for
    rerouting.
  - Closest design: Boschemeier and Mau (2023), one country's monthly imports after its
    partners' lockdowns. This is our template.
  - The gap, at the bottom: these papers study firms in large economies, usually after one
    shock. We found only one UAE paper (Ali et al. 2022), which measures concentration with no
    shocks.
- He may ask: "How are you different from Freund et al.?" Answer: they ask whether supply
  chains reshaped worldwide after one shock. We ask how one import-dependent country replaces
  lost supply across many shocks, and which suppliers win.

Slide 6. Data and our cut-offs (Adilet, 1.5 min)
- On the slide, a small table of the three sources, years and reliability:
  - BACI matches the UAE's own totals within about 10%.
  - Monthly supplier reports summed to years match BACI (ratio 0.99).
  And our cut-offs:
  - Big supplier: 50% or more of UAE imports of a product. This is the main rule.
  - Screen: we start from 20%, and show every result at 20, 30, 40 and 50%.
  - The supplier must pass the line in the year before and in one of the two years before that.
  - Drop: its share falls 15 points or more and its sales by at least a third.
  - Shock: has an official date (decree, sanctions order, disaster). We never pick one because
    imports fell.
- Say: "We show every cut-off so no result depends on one number we chose."
- He may ask: "Why trust what the suppliers report?" Answer: the UAE doesn't publish monthly
  data by country. Supplier reports match BACI when summed to years, so we use them for timing
  and BACI for levels.

Slide 7. What the data shows so far (Adilet, 2 min)
- On the slide: chart 10, plus two examples and the first look at each hypothesis.
  - Japan earthquake: car shipments to the UAE fell 74% in two months, and nobody filled the gap
    in those months.
  - India wheat ban: India fell from 43% of UAE wheat to almost zero. Russia gained 23 points.
  - H1: by the next year, others make up only 4-11% of the loss, at every cut-off.
  - H2: food sees about 24% made up, other goods about 4-5%.
  - H3: in 93% of drops, the biggest winner was already selling to the UAE.
  - H4: not tested yet.
- Say: "These are averages, not causal results. They tell us the question is real and the data
  can answer it."
- Don't claim that anything here is proven, or that H4 is tested.

Slide 8. Method, part 1: the economics and the event study (Adilet, 1.5 min)
- On the slide:
  - Intuition: buyers treat goods from different countries as close but not perfect
    substitutes, and switching has a cost. So replacement should be faster when products are
    similar (H2), slow overall (H1), and should go to known suppliers (H3).
  - Step 1, event study: for each shock, monthly imports from the hit supplier and from each
    alternative, 12 months before to 24 months after. Seasonal effects removed. Estimated with
    PPML so that months with zero imports stay in. This shows the loss, the recovery, and
    whether anything moved before the shock.
- He may ask: "Why PPML?" Answer: India's wheat to the UAE is exactly zero for a year. Logs
  would drop those months, and PPML keeps them.

Slide 9. Method, part 2: Gulf comparison and who replaces (Adilet for step 2, Sultan for
step 3, 1.5 min)
- On the slide:
  - Step 2, Gulf comparison (the causal step): the six Gulf countries depended on the hit
    supplier to different degrees. For example, India had 34% of UAE wheat but almost none of
    Saudi wheat. If alternatives grow more where dependence was bigger, the shock caused it. We
    run it at the product by country level, so each shock gives many observations. World
    importers are a backup check. H4 is tested here.
  - Step 3, who replaces: each alternative's gain in UAE share is explained by its earlier
    share, world exports, distance, trade agreements and price. This tests H3.
- He may ask: "Only six countries?" Answer: yes, that's our main weakness. We work at the
  product by country level, test whether trends differed before the shock, and check against
  world importers.

Slide 10. Flaws and how we handle them (both, 2 min, Adilet the first three, Sultan the rest)
- On the slide, short lines:
  - Six Gulf comparison countries is too few. How much each relied on the hit supplier is
    correlated with its earlier trends. Fix: product by country level, pre-trend tests, world
    check.
  - Some shocks were expected. India rushed wheat out before the ban bit, and onion exports
    fell after a duty in August 2023. Fix: date each shock at its first restriction.
  - Only six to ten shocks. Fix: case-by-case results and bootstrap inference.
  - Monthly data misses some suppliers (Korea before 2013, Russia after 2021). Fix: monthly
    replacement is a lower bound, and BACI covers the full picture yearly.
  - Rerouting (Bulgaria may be shipping Ukrainian oil) and re-exports through the UAE. Fix:
    check the winners' own production, and drop Oman as a check.
  - Our best case isn't a 50% case. Japan cars is 30% overall, and India only became a big
    wheat supplier in 2021. Fix: show every cut-off.
- Already checked:
  - The drops are bigger than in 98-100% of normal months.
  - In 6 of 7 cases the supplier sold less everywhere, so it was a supply problem, not the
    UAE choosing to buy less.
  - Quantities fell, not just prices.
- Say: "We'd rather tell you the weak points ourselves."
